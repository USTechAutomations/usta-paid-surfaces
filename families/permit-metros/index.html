Across those six cities in that week, 694 permits entered our copy and 52 sat on both copies with a different status word. Each city page below prints its own rows, its own counts and its own gaps, and rebuilds every day.

The Scottsdale row reached our copy on 17 Aug and the other five on 22 Aug, and that is not a mistake. Scottsdale’s board lands in one batch rather than a few permits a day: all 21 of its arrivals turned up in the copy we sealed on 17 Aug, and nothing has entered it in the five days since. Its page says so in those words, and recounts the days every time it rebuilds.

What entered is not what was issued today. A permit counts as entered when it is in the later sealed copy and was not in the earlier one. That is the day it reached us, not the day the city stamped it. Of those 694, 525 carry a city issue date on or after 15 Aug 2026 and 169 were issued before it and only reached the board afterwards. Both numbers are on every city page, because one without the other reads better than it is.

Nothing left the list, and that is a finding

People expect a change feed to show things leaving. This one does not, and we would rather say why than quietly leave the column out.

We compared every pair of copies we hold: 771 pairs across all 12 boards. In none of them is there a permit that was in one copy and not in the next. These boards add and they update, and once a permit is on them it stays. So the honest product here is what entered and what changed status, and the count for anything leaving is zero, printed as zero.

Doing this yourself

Each city publishes today’s issued-permit file and overwrites yesterday’s. To know what entered last week you have to read all six boards on the same day every week, keep the old copies somewhere you can still open them, and line up six different file layouts by permit number.

Miss a day and that day is gone. The city does not republish it and there is no public archive to go back to. We have 783 dated copies going back to 7 Jun 2026, holding 1,720,069 sealed permit rows between them.

What this page cannot tell you

This is the city’s published board, not the city’s whole record. We hold what the city put on its public permit file on the day we read it. A permit the city never published is not here, and we cannot tell you how many of those there are.

In our copy is not the same as issued today. A permit counted as new here is one that is in our newest copy and was not in the one before it. That is first seen by us. Some of them carry a city issue date older than our earlier copy, and each page below says how many.

Nobody who owns a building is named. These pages name the permit, the address and, where the city gives one, the contractor. They do not name a property owner, and we would not add one if we could.

The status words are each city’s own. We print them exactly as the copy sealed them and we do not translate them, so one city’s words cannot be lined up against another city’s.

Questions people ask before they pay

This data is public and free. Why pay you for it?

You are not paying for the data. You are paying for last week’s copy of it.

Every one of these boards shows what is current and overwrites the rest. Fetch Austin now and you get Austin now. There is nowhere to fetch Austin last Friday. We have been sealing a dated copy of each board, so the question “what entered this week” still has an answer. If you already keep your own daily copies, you do not need us.

Do you tell me who is building it?

In four of the six cities, sometimes. In two, never.

New York City puts a contractor on every permit we hold. Austin does on 93% of them and Scottsdale on 94%. Seattle does on 7%. Chicago and San Francisco publish a house number and a street and no builder at all, so those two pages can tell you where the job is and not who is on it. Where a city leaves the field blank, the cell reads “not given” rather than being filled in.

Do you name the property owner?

No. Not on any page here, and not in any file we send.

New York’s own public permit file carries the owner’s name and our collector never copies it across. These pages name the permit, the address and, where the city gives one, the contractor. If you need owner names, we are not the supplier.

Are you allowed to collect this?

Every board here is an open-data file the city publishes itself for anyone to read.

Eleven of the twelve come from the city’s own open-data service, read through the public interface it publishes for that purpose, by a collector that says who it is. Scottsdale is read from the city’s own public permit search. We do not log in anywhere, we do not pretend to be a browser, and where a source tells us to stop we stop on the day we find out and say so on the page.

If the metro you need is not on that list, we do not hold it. Email us and we will tell you, in the reply, rather than start collecting it and bill you for the wait.
