What the signal actually is

A satellite takes a picture of the same fixed square of ground at each of 57 sites. Some of that square reads as bare ground — soil, gravel, concrete, a graded pad. Some of it reads as plants, water or cloud. Put two of our dated pictures of the same square side by side and the share reading as bare ground has either moved or it has not.

That is all of it. There is no clever part. The work is keeping the pictures dated, week after week, so that in November you can still prove what the ground looked like in June.

This is pictures, not permits. Nothing in this feed is a filing, an approval, an announcement or a contract. If you need to know why the ground changed, the county and the company are where you ask. We can hand you the two dated pictures that disagree.

We publish no megawatt figure and no percent-built figure. Ever. Neither of those things is in a satellite picture. Printing one next to a picture reading would be inventing it, and it is the single easiest lie for a feed like this to tell, so we have shut the door on it rather than trusted ourselves near it.

A move of five percentage points is our line, and we chose it. Across the 372 pairs of pictures we can compare, 281 move by less than five points and 91 move by five or more. Below five points a reading is inside the ordinary week-to-week wobble of this measure, so we do not call it a change. The number is on every page so you can check our arithmetic.

What a change in bare ground does not mean

The square is bigger than the site. Every picture covers the same fixed square, 65,536 pixels of it, centred on the site. That square takes in whatever else is around — fields, roads, a car park, a neighbour’s land. A move here is a move across the whole square. It is not a measurement inside a fence line, because we do not have the fence line.

Summer moves this reading on its own. Bare ground falls when grass and crops grow back, and rises when a field beside the site is harvested or ploughed. We do not separate weather and farming from work on the site, and we will not pretend to. On a site ringed by farmland, read a summer fall as the season before you read it as anything else.

Ground getting barer is not building going up. From this height, fresh concrete, cleared soil and a ploughed field look much alike. And a flat reading does not mean an idle site: steel going up inside a pad that was already cleared, or a fit-out under a finished roof, leaves this reading exactly where it was.

A new picture every week is not a change every week. Every one of our 614 pictures is a different file from the last one, because it is a different pass on a different day. That proves nothing on its own. Only the reading moving proves anything, and only above our five-point line.

Doing this yourself

The pictures are free and public. Anyone can go and get today’s. What you cannot go and get is the unbroken weekly run of them for a fixed list of sites, read the same way every week, starting on a day before anybody knew which of those sites would matter.

To build this yourself you would have to pick the sites and freeze the list, then read every one of them every week without missing a week, then decide what counts as a real move and hold that decision still. The reading is the easy half. Not changing your mind about the method halfway through is the work.
