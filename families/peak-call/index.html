Read this before anything else

A big electricity bill is not really made of the electricity you used. A large part of it is set by how much you were pulling during a few hours of the year — the hours when the whole grid was at its busiest. Miss those hours and you pay for them all year.

So the useful thing is not a warning. It is a warning that turns out to be right. Anyone can send warnings every week and be right eventually; every wrong one costs you production. This page exists so that when we do have a hit rate, it will cover a stretch that started before we ever tried to sell anything.

The line at the top of this page says “sample not ready”, and that is right. Other feeds here hand you a slice of a file to look at before you buy it. There is no file here and nothing to buy: the record below is the product, and all of it is on the page.

What a call isread from the recorder · the New York state grid

Once a day, for the day after, we look at the grid operator’s own published forecast of how busy the whole system will be, and we write down one of two things: cut your load, or ordinary day. That is the call. It is one line, it is about one day, and it is written before that day happens.

We only say cut your load when the forecast sits near the very top of what the grid has actually done recently, and “near the top” is a written rule rather than a judgement on the day: 90% of the last 60 recorded days. Saying it rarely is the whole point — a warning you get every fortnight is one you learn to ignore.

No call is made at all until the record behind that rule is long enough to justify one: the recorder wants at least 10 days of real grid load on file first, and says so instead of guessing.

The call is sealed the moment it is made, before anybody knows how the day went. Each one is stamped with a fingerprint worked out from the call itself, so if we ever went back and changed one, its fingerprint would stop matching. How a call is scored

After the day is over, the grid operator publishes what the system actually did. We compare that with what we said, and the call gets one of these words. They come out of the scoring code itself, so this list cannot drift away from the rule it describes.

  • correct quiet day — rightWe said it would be an ordinary day, and it was.
  • false alarm — wrongWe said cut, and the day turned out ordinary. This is the expensive one: it costs real production and buys nothing.
  • hit — rightWe said cut, and the day really was near the top of the record.
  • miss — wrongWe said it would be an ordinary day, and it was near the top. You were not warned.

The wrong ones stay on the page. A false alarm is the expensive mistake — you cut production for nothing — so it is counted out loud rather than quietly dropped out of the total.

The number we are not printing yet

We do not have a hit rate, so there is no percentage on this page. No call has told anyone to cut yet, so there is nothing to be right or wrong about.

The bar was written down before the record was anywhere near it. The recorder will not treat this record as worth selling until at least 60 calls have been scored and at least 5 of them told somebody to cut. A percentage off a handful of calls flatters whoever prints it, which is why the bar was set while the answer was still no.

When there is a percentage, it will be on this line, and it will have the wrong calls inside it.
